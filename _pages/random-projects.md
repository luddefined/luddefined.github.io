# Short Films/Openings

This means calling this section "short films" is slightly misleading, however many do not deny the cinema that is both of these productions.

{% include video id="1j1TJ-66mIEAkyXt4cqJ4_6cumYRWRhl_" provider="google-drive" %}

Written and directed alongside someone who would then many years later produce the Tea Time soundtrack, as well as them featuring as the main character of this opening, we both agree that it is the most David Lynch-esque thing we've created despite not knowing much about his work at the time of creation.

## Bodys (late 2022)

{% include video id="1nRMj77-rqD7ko1yoD7NSdyWHP-yZbX1v" provider="google-drive" %}

The writers hasn't changed since the previous year's project, now with a new goal of creating a black comedy which answers the age of question of "where would you hide the body"? 


# Roblox-related

![image-center](/assets/images/other/roblox-1.jpg){: .align-center}

Roblox happens to be a very core component of my life, with its various games providing avenues to explore creating (Roblox) models. It's also where I learnt to program, following various online tutorials as well as a plugin dedicated to teaching Roblox development. Although some of these projects no longer exist, their presence can be felt in everything I've made since then. 

## Models (2018-2021)

These models are submissions for the once-popular game [*Miner's Haven*](https://www.roblox.com/games/258258996/Miners-Haven-Sandbox-Tycoon). Instead of just having ideas on content for the game and living with them for the rest of my life, I could create physical versions of them with Roblox Studio's very simple modelling system. I mostly relied on Roblox's default primitive shapes (cubes, wedges, etc.), though I occasionally dabbled with Blender to produce something with more than 6 faces. Some of these items were made by myself, though there are a couple of times I had collaborated with others as well.

## Platformer (2018)

Alongside these models, there was also a submission for a map in the somewhat-popular-but-only-by-it's-name game [*Flood Escape 2*](https://www.roblox.com/games/738339342/Flood-Escape-2). This was the true primordial platformer, the one which inspired all the others then on. Built with overwhelming confidence from hearing about vague concepts of "level design" and "flow", and with a couple hours locked the level editor of the very popular non-Roblox game *Geometry Dash*, I ended up building an acidic-themed level with an emphasis on the game's swimming mechanic.

# Tabletop Game (2025)

![image-center](/assets/images/other/tabletop-0.jpg){: .align-center}

Now here's a long one. Our university course's "big assignment" of the second year was an assignment to design and develop a tabletop game in large groups. The group I was assigned with ended up developing one of *the* tabletop games of all time, with an equally perplexing story to go alongside its development. Though I won't be explaining what went on in that story, know that each of us were surprised we managed to get the game out, which should be a *fantastic* indicator for its quality.

*Elemental Hunt* follows the path carved by other deck-building and roguelike games. It's a 4-player race to be the first one to defeat five 'boss' enemies, gathering ability cards and dice along the way. Each player is assigned one of the three classical elements plus lighting (I have no* idea how this was not the four classical elements we agreed upon), which corresponds to their starting ability cards and dice types they can use. Round are setup such that players advance across a map by defeating the number of enemies equivalent to the distance they wish to travel, and spending time in-between rounds at the shop to get new cards and dice with gold obtained from enemies. The first player to defeat five 'boss' type enemies win, so the general strategy focuses on blocking the paths players wish to choose by taking risks, and buying out ability cards and dice they wish to use. 

For a game that is essentially a compromise of every major idea that some team members had, it is a surprisungly engaging experience. Perhaps not entirely faultless in some ways as we have not extensively tested how far players can break things, but given some of the dire designs our team had initially pursued, I think this is a fair trade (it's either this or an earlier concept I had playtested with others who described the game as "effectively watching dice play the game itself"). In many ways, the development story is a fairytale about the importance of playtesting and proper communication. 

Now with the awareness and understanding of the intricacies of tabletop game development, I think one of those grander original concepts could be revisted and potentially reworked into a fully fledged game. With every module did our lecturers warn about the evil tales of overscoping and *'one not knowing of their limits'*, but after reflecting over this assignment - the planning and design work we all contributed - I think that if another opportunity to design and develop another tabletop game ever arises, at the very least I'm bringing along a dozen of ideas and concept cards.
